# Editorial Style Spec — Leave-Behind Briefs

The reusable standard. Governs the leave-behind document. Register 4 (narrative) voice with Tier 2 integrity guardrails.

---

## 1. The One Rule Above All

**Every section title states a conclusion, not a topic.** A heading titled "Data Governance" fails; "Governed once — but the governance doesn't travel" passes. If a heading could be a chapter name, it's wrong; if it's a sentence you'd say out loud in a hallway, it's right. Pass the "so what?" test on every headline.

## 2. Voice — Register 4 (Narrative)

- **Structural POV:** Amazon 6-pager / working-backwards. Prose memo, no bullets unless listing discrete items. Start from the customer outcome and reason back. Section headers tell the story when read alone.
- **Prose texture:** Stripe — plain, direct, unpretentious. Sentences a smart reader respects. No slide-ware shortcuts, no marketing adjectives, no hedge-stacking.
- **Headline test:** "Does this read as a considered argument, with section headers that carry the spine on their own?"

### What Register 4 is NOT:
- NOT Register 1 (editorial/deck voice) — briefs don't need antithesis devices or Economist understatement. They need clarity and directness.
- NOT a slide export with prose padding between screenshots.
- NOT an email (too short, too action-focused) or a report (too long, too comprehensive).

## 3. Integrity Guardrails (Tier 2 — All Customer-Facing)

- **Never name internal stakeholders on the page.** Reference roles ("your platform team," "the analytics director"), never names. Names belong in internal-only documents.
- **Never make the incumbent tool the enemy.** If they just migrated to it, it coexists. The villain is always a property of the architecture (fragmentation, governance gaps, latency), never a tool or a team's choice.
- **No vendor attack.** Competitors named only as neutral mechanisms, in partner-diagnosis tone, never as the bad guy.
- **Numbers are modeled, never claimed.** "Modeled on your actual revenue and bed count — logic walkable." Never "you will save $X." Internal tool names or analyst names NEVER cross into customer-facing language; credibility comes from their numbers, not our people.
- **No shadow-IT / Excel shaming.** Finance teams live in Excel; never frame it as the problem.
- **Affirm the win before any critique (IKEA effect).** Name what they built well, then redirect. Critique lands as "you've already sensed this," never "you did this wrong."

## 4. Numbers Discipline

- Distinguish a total anchor from per-item splits. If only a total is defensible, show only the total; per-quadrant splits that would be fabricated are forbidden.
- Lead with the heavyweight figure, let it sit. Conservative framing > inflation — sophisticated readers detect inflation instantly and it costs all your credibility.
- Source every number: "(modeled from FY24 claims data)" or "(industry benchmark, McKinsey 2024)". Unsourced numbers read as made up.

## 5. Builder's-Frame Close

End on a decision the customer controls, framed as generous expansion, never gatekeeping. "Deciding how widely to open it" (hand on the aperture), never "who gets access" (bouncer at the rope).

The ask should be specific and low-friction: a named next step, a timeline, a concrete thing they can say yes to without committee.

## 6. Visual System (Inline Figures)

- Figures are small and inline — half-column width, never full-bleed.
- One-line caption below each figure. Caption is a quiet label, not a second headline.
- Export hero exhibits from deck as PNG if available; otherwise describe what the figure shows and mark `[FIGURE: description]` for later placement.
- No shadows, gradients, glow, decorative borders on figures.
- Maximum 2-3 figures in a 2-3 page brief. More than that and the exhibit is doing too much work — the prose should carry the argument.

## 7. Length and Pacing

- **2-3 pages.** Reads in five minutes. If it's longer, it's a report — cut. If shorter, the argument isn't developed.
- **4-6 sections.** Each section earns its place by advancing the spine.
- **Front-load the insight.** The first section should make the reader think "they understand our situation" within 30 seconds. Don't throat-clear.
- **End cleanly.** The last section is 3-4 sentences: the ask, the timeline, the open door. No summaries, no "in conclusion."
